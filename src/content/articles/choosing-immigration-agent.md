A licensed agent is not legally required for the Nomad Residence Permit. You can submit directly through the Residency Malta portal yourself — and at the right risk profile (clean Schengen history, straightforward foreign employment, low-friction origin country), DIY application is workable. (This is different from Malta's Permanent Residence Programme, where a licensed agent is mandatory.)

But most successful NRP applicants engage an agent anyway. The reason is risk reduction, not regulatory compliance. The Residency Malta Agency maintains a published list of licensed agents on its website, and the firms on that list range from large multi-jurisdictional law firms to specialist immigration practices to two-person consultancies. Their fees vary by a factor of three. Their actual quality of service varies by considerably more.

You will not find an honest comparison of these firms anywhere. The firms themselves cannot publish one — comparing your direct competitors is a fast track to professional reputation problems. Industry directories that claim to rank them are usually selling featured placements. Forum threads where applicants compare experiences are useful but fragmented and out of date.

This guide gives you a framework for choosing well. It does not name names — the listing of recommended firms lives in the [directory](/directory) where partner relationships are disclosed. The framework here is independent of which firm you ultimately pick.

## Should you engage an agent at all?

Skip the rest of this article if your case is clean: stable foreign employment with a non-Maltese employer, clean three-year Schengen history, no AML or sanctions exposure, no criminal record, country of origin not on Malta's high-risk list, and no complex family situation. The €1,500–€3,000 agent fee is real money for low marginal benefit on a clean application.

Engage an agent if any of these are true: prior Schengen visa denial in the past five years, family member or business partner with potential AML exposure, complex corporate ownership structure, country of origin where apostille processing is slow or document quality is variable, prior Maltese tax history, mixed income sources where the €42K threshold needs careful documentation, or you simply prefer to outsource the process and have someone else manage the timeline.

The cost of a rejected application is roughly €19,000–€25,000 in already-committed costs (lease, document prep, flights, etc.) plus a 12-month wait before reapplying. Against that, an agent fee of €1,500–€3,000 is the cheapest insurance available — but only if your case has genuine risk factors. For a clean profile, you're paying for peace of mind, not for outcome change.

## The first realisation

Most applicants who do engage an agent approach the selection backwards. They look at fee. They pick the cheapest one. They get what they pay for.

The right starting point is to understand what you're actually buying.

You are not buying "form filing." Filing the NRP application is a 90-minute task on the Residency Malta portal. Anyone who can read English and fill in a web form can do it.

You are buying four things, in approximate order of importance:

**Pre-submission risk reduction.** A good agent runs your file against the [seven rejection patterns](/guides/nrp-rejection-reasons) and tells you what to fix before you've paid the €300 government fee. A bad agent submits whatever you give them and shrugs if it gets rejected.

**Document preparation guidance.** A good agent has a checklist customised for your origin country and tells you exactly which documents you need, in which order, with which apostille requirements. A bad agent gives you the generic Residency Malta document list and lets you figure out the rest.

**Government relationship.** A good agent has direct relationships with case officers at Residency Malta. When the Agency requests additional information, the agent translates the request into something practical and responds promptly. A bad agent forwards the email and waits for you to respond.

**Post-approval continuity.** A good agent handles the lease registration, the biometrics booking, the bank introduction, and the renewal cycle. A bad agent disappears after the residence card is issued and reappears wanting another full fee for the renewal.

The fee differential between a good agent and a bad agent is often smaller than the cost of a single rejected application. Optimise for quality, not price.

## The seven dimensions to evaluate

Below is the framework I'd use to evaluate any agent. None of these dimensions is decisive on its own. Together they give you a usable picture.

### 1. Licensed status

Every legitimate agent appears on the Residency Malta Agency's published agents list. The list is the authoritative source — verify any firm you're considering against it before doing anything else.

This is the most basic check and the easiest one to skip. Verify before any other consideration.

If a firm presenting itself as an immigration agent does not appear on the official list, walk away — even if their service offer looks attractive. Working with an unlicensed firm exposes you to two specific risks: their work product may not be accepted by the Agency on procedural grounds, and you have no professional-conduct recourse if something goes wrong.

### 2. Licence age and case volume

Licences are not all created equal. A firm that has held a licence for ten years and processed several thousand applications has institutional memory of edge cases that a one-year-old firm with twelve applications cannot match.

Ask, directly: how many NRP applications has your firm submitted in the past 24 months? What is your approval rate? How many of your rejections did you successfully reapply for after twelve months?

Reputable agents will give you these numbers. Evasive ones will pivot to "every case is different." That pivot tells you something.

The case-volume metric matters more than the firm's overall age. A 50-year-old law firm with a brand-new immigration practice is less helpful than a 5-year-old immigration specialist with hundreds of NRP applications under its belt.

### 3. Fee structure and what it includes

There are three common fee structures, each with different incentive properties:

**Fixed fee, all-inclusive.** A single number covers preliminary due diligence, application preparation, submission, post-approval support through biometrics, and accommodation guidance. Typically €2,000–€4,000 for a single applicant.

**Fixed fee, application-only.** Covers the application work but excludes anything after Letter of Approval in Principle. Typically €1,000–€2,500. Cheaper headline number but you'll pay separately for everything that comes after.

**Hourly billing.** Rare for NRP work but exists at premium law firms. Typically €200–€350 per hour. Avoid unless you have a complex case where the firm's senior partners are genuinely needed.

The fixed-fee, all-inclusive structure is almost always the right choice. The headline price is higher but the total cost across the full process is usually lower because the agent's incentives are aligned with closing your case efficiently rather than billing extra hours.

What to confirm is in scope:

- Preliminary due diligence (yes — this should be standard)
- Document checklist tailored to your origin country (yes)
- Translation of letter of intent (sometimes; sometimes extra)
- Liaison with Maltese tax authority for tax registration number (yes, ideally)
- Lease registration with Housing Authority (sometimes extra; budget €100–€200)
- Biometrics booking and accompaniment (yes)
- Bank introduction (sometimes; sometimes extra)
- Renewal application year 2 (almost always extra; this is where price-shopping pays off)

Always get the scope in writing. Always ask for a written quote covering the full path from preliminary check to residence card issuance.

### 4. Response time on first contact

This is the single most predictive metric for what your client experience will be like.

Email three or four agents with a substantively similar enquiry. Use a real address and a brief, specific question — for example, "I'm a [your nationality] software engineer earning €70K from a US tech company. I'd like to apply for the Maltese NRP. Can you confirm I qualify and provide a fee quote?"

Then measure:

- How long until you get a substantive response (not an automated acknowledgment)
- Whether the response actually answers your specific question or pivots to "we need to schedule a call"
- Whether the firm's response addresses your nationality-specific risks (Schengen denials common to your origin? Apostille slow from your country? AML enhanced due diligence likely?)
- Whether the response includes a specific fee quote or stays vague
- Who responds — a senior practitioner, a junior associate, or a sales person

Firms that respond within 24 hours with substantive, specific answers are dramatically easier to work with than firms that take a week to schedule a "discovery call." This is also a leading indicator for how they'll handle the more important communications during the actual application — the document request from the Agency, the rejection threat that needs a fast response, the renewal-year deadline.

### 5. Native-language capability

If you're applying from a country where English is not your first language, ask whether the firm has staff who speak your native language fluently.

This matters for two reasons. First, technical immigration vocabulary is hard to navigate in a second language and even small misunderstandings can cause real problems. Second, the document preparation step often involves coordinating with notaries, translators, and apostille offices in your origin country — having an agent who can have those conversations directly accelerates the process by weeks.

The firms with the strongest native-language coverage are typically the ones with explicit international clientele:

- Russian, Ukrainian, Belarusian (post-Soviet client base) — common, though Russia/Belarus applications are currently blocked
- Mandarin — increasingly common
- Arabic — limited but growing
- Hindi, Urdu — limited
- Portuguese, Spanish — common at firms with Latin American or Iberian focus

If your native language is uncommon, you're choosing between a firm that handles your language poorly and a firm that handles it not at all. The first is meaningfully better.

### 6. Conflict-of-interest management

Many Maltese immigration firms also offer related services — property purchase advisory, MPRP application work, citizenship services, tax advisory. These can be useful integrated offerings but also create conflicts of interest.

The honest question to ask: if I apply for the NRP and at year 2 you tell me I should upgrade to MPRP, how should I trust that recommendation given that MPRP earns you a much larger fee?

A reputable agent will acknowledge the conflict and explain how they manage it. Common approaches:

- Separating the immigration team from the MPRP team
- Quoting fees in advance for both paths so you can see the bias
- Refusing to upsell during the first 18 months unless the client raises it themselves
- Disclosing referral relationships with property developers, banks, accountants

A less reputable agent will dismiss the question or give a vague reassurance. The dismissal itself is informative.

### 7. Renewal and lifecycle support

The NRP is not a one-shot application. It's a four-year relationship that includes three renewal applications, an annual tax filing, and a final exit decision (MPRP upgrade, citizenship route through another country, or onward visa to a different EU state).

A good agent prices this lifecycle from the start. They tell you what year 2 will cost. They retain your file and don't make you re-prepare documents from scratch each year. They flag rule changes that might affect your renewal before you discover them yourself.

A bad agent treats each year as a fresh transaction. They charge full initial-application fees for renewals. They don't alert you to changes in the eligibility criteria. They lose your file between years.

The fee difference between a lifecycle-priced relationship and a transactional one is meaningful: roughly €4,000–€8,000 across the full four years, depending on family size.

Always ask about year 2, 3, 4 fees before signing for year 1.

## A practical evaluation script

Here is the email I would send to three or four candidate agents at the start of the selection process. Adjust for your own situation.

> Subject: NRP application enquiry — [your nationality], remote tech work, EUR [your income]
>
> Hello,
>
> I'm exploring the Malta Nomad Residence Permit and would like to evaluate your firm as a potential agent.
>
> Brief profile:
> - Nationality: [country]
> - Current residence: [country]
> - Income: approximately EUR [number] per year, paid by [employer type and country] for [your role]
> - Family: [single, couple, or family with kids and ages]
> - Schengen visa history: [clean / specify any prior denials]
> - Target application timeline: [month/quarter]
>
> Could you please:
>
> 1. Confirm whether my profile presents any issues you can identify at this stage
> 2. Provide a written fee quote covering the application through to residence card collection, with a separate line for renewal year fees
> 3. Confirm your firm's current listing on the Residency Malta agents register and approximate NRP application volume in the past 24 months
> 4. Confirm what is included in the fee (preliminary due diligence, document checklist, lease registration, biometrics support, etc.)
>
> Happy to schedule a call after I've had a chance to review your written response. I'm in conversation with two other firms in parallel.
>
> Best regards,
> [Your name]

Two specific things that email does:

It says "I'm in conversation with two other firms in parallel." This is true and it changes the response you get. Firms that know they're competing put their best response forward.

It asks for written information before agreeing to a call. This filters out the firms whose business model is built around getting you onto a sales call before showing you anything specific. Those firms tend to be the ones with weaker actual capabilities.

## Red flags

Beyond the dimensions above, these are signals that strongly suggest you should pick a different agent:

**No written quote provided.** "We discuss fees on the call." This is a sales tactic. Walk away.

**A guarantee of approval.** No legitimate agent guarantees approval. The Agency's discretion is real and explicit in the regulations. Firms that promise "100% success rate" or "guaranteed approval" are misrepresenting either their record or the rules.

**Pressure to commit quickly.** "We can submit this week if you wire the fee today" is a sales script. The actual application timeline is 30–60 days; there is no urgency that justifies skipping due diligence.

**Refusal to confirm their listing on the Residency Malta agents register.** Trivial to verify, free to provide. Refusal is informative.

**A request to open a Maltese bank account in their name to "facilitate the application."** No legitimate process requires this. Application fees come from your own account, period. This pattern is a fraud signal.

**Fees significantly below market.** The €1,500–€3,000 range for single-applicant fees reflects what serious firms need to charge to deliver the service properly. €500 quotes typically mean either the firm is not actually doing the work, or they're using your application as a loss-leader to upsell more expensive services later.

## A note on tier-1 law firms vs specialist immigration consultancies

Maltese immigration work is split between two kinds of providers:

**Tier-1 law firms.** Established multi-practice firms (typically including tax, corporate, property, and immigration) that handle NRP work as one service line among many. Higher fees, more institutional weight, longer response times, often less hand-holding on the practical relocation steps.

**Specialist immigration consultancies.** Firms whose primary practice is residency and citizenship work. Lower fees, faster response, more practical guidance, sometimes weaker if your case has unusual legal complexity (corporate structure issues, prior immigration violations, complex family situations).

For a typical NRP applicant — clean profile, salaried or freelance remote work, no prior issues — the specialist consultancy is usually the better choice. Better service, lower cost, and the legal complexity that justifies a tier-1 firm's premium isn't actually present in most cases.

For applicants with complex cases — prior visa denials, complicated business ownership structures, AML exposure, family situations involving custody disputes — the tier-1 firm's broader expertise is worth paying for.

The mistake is choosing on prestige rather than fit. The largest firm with the most impressive office is not always the best choice for your specific application.

## How to make the final decision

After evaluating three or four candidates against the framework above, the choice usually clarifies itself. One firm responds substantively, quotes transparently, has the right licence and case volume, speaks your language, and handles your specific risk profile.

If two firms feel roughly equal, two final tiebreakers help:

**Talk to a recent client.** Reputable firms can connect you with a former client willing to discuss their experience, or point you at a public testimonial that's verifiable. The conversation matters more than the testimonial copy.

**Check whether they offer a no-fault refund.** Some agents will refund the bulk of their fee if the application is rejected for reasons within their control (document errors, missed deadlines, wrong submissions). The presence of this offer signals confidence in their work and aligns incentives.

The agent decision is one you make once and live with for four years. Spend the time. The €1,500 difference between the cheapest agent and the right agent is rounding error in the context of a €20,000+ relocation. The actual difference in outcome is much larger.
