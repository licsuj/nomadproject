If you're reading this around month 8 or 9 of your first NRP year, you're at exactly the right time to start thinking about renewal. Most people don't.

The application stage is well-covered. Law firms write detailed pages on it because that's where their leads come from. The renewal stage is barely covered anywhere — partly because the same firms have already collected their fee and moved on, partly because the rules are spread across three different documents that don't reference each other, and partly because most permit holders only realise they're doing the wrong thing when the renewal deadline is two weeks away.

This article is the pre-emptive version. It assumes you have a permit and you're trying to figure out what month 12 actually looks like.

## The 5-cumulative-months rule, in practice

Here's the rule, verbatim from Residency Malta: to be eligible for renewal, you must provide "a bank statement showing payment transactions carried out in Malta as proof that you have resided in Malta for a cumulative period of at least five (5) months over the previous twelve (12) months."

The phrasing matters more than people realise. Three things to understand:

**Cumulative, not consecutive.** You don't have to be in Malta for five months in a row. You can leave, come back, leave again. The five months are added up across the year. So a pattern of "two weeks in Malta, one week travelling, two weeks in Malta" is fine, as long as it adds up.

**Five months out of twelve, not five-and-a-half or six.** Some agents tell their clients to plan for 6+ months "to be safe." That's defensive advice, not the actual rule. Five months is the legal floor. If you're at exactly five months and one day, you qualify.

**The proof is bank-statement evidence.** This is the critical part most permit holders miss until they're already non-compliant. Residency Malta isn't checking your passport stamps. They're checking your Maltese bank statements (or the Maltese-side activity of whatever bank account you use) for transactions that prove you were physically here. So the relevant question becomes: what counts as a "transaction in Malta"?

## What counts as evidence of presence

The Agency doesn't publish a definitive list. Based on what licensed agents recommend and what successful renewal files include, the working understanding is:

- **Card payments at Maltese merchants** (supermarkets, restaurants, pharmacies, Tallinja Card top-ups). These are unambiguous proof of presence and the strongest form of evidence.
- **ATM withdrawals from Maltese ATMs.** Useful but harder to interpret on their own — a withdrawal might just mean someone is using your card.
- **Maltese utility payments** (ARMS for water/electricity, Melita or GO for internet) coming out of your account. These prove you have an active home setup, which supports presence even if you weren't physically in Malta on the day of the payment.
- **Maltese rent payments** from your account to your landlord. Useful but, again, payments don't prove presence on a given day.
- **Direct deposits or withdrawals at Maltese branches.** Strong evidence if you do them.

What doesn't count:
- **Wise transactions** that don't show as Maltese-side activity. Wise is borderless by design, so a Wise transaction can happen anywhere in the world. The Agency has no way of knowing where you were.
- **Online purchases** from international retailers (Amazon, etc.).
- **Foreign card transactions** that happen to settle in EUR.

The cleanest way to handle this is to open a Maltese bank account, use a Maltese debit card for daily expenses, and let the bank statement do the work. Most permit holders use Wise or Revolut day-to-day and only bother opening a Maltese account when their renewal looms. By then, they don't have a year of evidence.

If you're at month 6 or earlier and haven't opened a local account, this is the single highest-leverage thing you can do for your renewal. If you're at month 9+ and haven't, the next-best move is to start using your Wise card heavily at Maltese merchants, document each transaction (location matters, not just amount), and supplement with airline boarding passes and Maltese utility statements as backup.

## The renewal timing window

Submit the renewal application **between two and three months before your current permit expires.**

Why this window:
- Submitting too early (more than three months out) gets your application rejected on procedural grounds. The Agency wants the renewal evidence to reflect the actual permit year, which means most of the year needs to have passed first.
- Submitting too late (less than two months out, or god forbid after expiry) creates a gap. Your current permit expires before the renewal is issued, and during the gap your immigration status is irregular. Any application submitted after expiry is automatically refused.

The Agency processes renewal applications within roughly the same 30-working-day window as initial applications. So if you submit at exactly the two-month mark, you'll have your Letter of Approval in Principle a few days before your old card expires. The card itself takes another 3-4 weeks after that. There will likely be a brief period where you have approval in principle but no new card yet — you can stay in Malta during this window because your status is regular.

The practical playbook: set a calendar reminder for nine months after your card was issued. That's when you start gathering the bank statement evidence and reviewing your file. Submit between months 9 and 10. The renewal lands in time.

## What you have to re-prove

Most of the original application requirements still apply, but with a few important differences for renewals:

**Income evidence.** You re-submit three months of bank statements showing income at €3,500/month minimum, plus your continuing employment contract or service agreements. If your income has dropped below €42,000 annual equivalent, your renewal can be denied — but in practice the Agency assesses against the threshold that applied when you originally applied. So if you applied before 1 April 2024, the lower €32,400 threshold still applies to your renewals.

**Employment status documents.** Your current employment contract, freelance agreements, or self-employment evidence. If anything has changed since the original application — new employer, new clients, new business structure — you submit the updated documents. If nothing has changed, you re-submit the same evidence.

**Police conduct certificate.** Required at renewal as well as at initial application. The certificate must be original, less than 6 months old at the time of renewal submission, and translated to English if not already. Some countries (Australia, New Zealand, USA, UK, Canada) don't require apostille/legalisation; others do. Plan a 4–8 week lead time to obtain it, depending on your country.

**Tax compliance declaration (second and third renewals only).** From renewal year two onwards, Residency Malta requires a signed declaration confirming you've complied with applicable tax laws and reporting obligations. The form is downloadable from the Residency Malta portal. This requirement does NOT apply at your first renewal — only at the second and third.

**Health insurance.** A renewed annual policy meeting Malta's €100,000 minimum coverage. Submit it at the Letter of Approval in Principle stage, not at initial submission. UK nationals remain exempt.

**Accommodation.** Your existing 12-month lease, registered with the Housing Authority, that covers the new permit period. If your lease is up for renewal close to the permit renewal, time it so you have an unbroken accommodation chain.

**The new piece: presence evidence.** This is the only requirement that's substantially new at renewal. Your bank statement(s) showing the cumulative five months of Maltese transactions. This is the document most renewal denials hinge on.

## The tax filing nobody warns you about

This is the section most renewal-year articles don't include and most permit holders find out about by accident.

When your residence card was issued, you were **automatically registered** for income tax in Malta. This happens silently — you don't get a confirmation email, no one walks you through it, the registration just happens behind the scenes through the MTCA (Malta Tax and Customs Administration). The MTCA Guidelines published 16 January 2026 confirm this is now the standard process for all post-January-2024 permit holders.

What this means at renewal time:

**Year one:** Your authorised work income is exempt from Maltese tax for the first 12 months. Most people don't realise this exemption requires you to file a tax return anyway, declaring the income and claiming the exemption. You can't passively use the exemption — you have to actively claim it. The deadline is the standard Maltese tax return deadline (typically 30 June for the previous calendar year, but check the current MTCA calendar).

**Year two onwards:** Your authorised work income is taxed at a flat 10%. You file a Maltese tax return reporting the income, and the 10% is applied. Other income (Maltese rental income, capital gains, side projects with Maltese clients) is taxed normally on the progressive scale.

**The catch:** if you've spent more than 183 days in Malta in a calendar year, you may be considered Maltese tax-resident for that year, which extends your tax obligations to your worldwide income (with double taxation relief available). Permit holders meeting the 5-month minimum often inadvertently cross the 183-day threshold without realising it.

The practical move: engage a Maltese tax accountant in month 11 of your first year. Cost is roughly €600–€1,200 annually for a straightforward NRP filing. Don't try to file the Maltese return yourself unless you read Maltese tax legislation for fun. The 10% flat rate sounds simple; the actual filing involves Form FS3, Form TD3, double taxation declarations, and registration confirmation paperwork that an accountant will get right and you probably won't.

## Family member alignment

If your spouse or children are on the permit as dependents, all permits expire on the same date — yours. There's no separate timeline. You renew the main applicant's permit, and the family members are renewed as dependents on the same application.

What changes between renewal and initial application for family members:
- Birth certificates and marriage certificates were already submitted with the original application; no need to re-submit.
- Family members are not separately required to provide presence evidence (only the main applicant is). But if a spouse has been physically absent for most of the year, the Agency may notice and ask questions.
- Dependent children turning 18 between the original application and a renewal need to be re-evaluated as adult dependents — they need to demonstrate continued financial dependence on the main applicant.

If your family situation has changed significantly (divorce, separation, an adult child becoming financially independent), the renewal is also the moment to update Residency Malta. They'll work out which family members continue as dependents and which need to be removed from the permit.

## What gets renewals denied

Initial applications and renewals fail for different reasons. The five most common renewal denial patterns:

**1. Insufficient presence evidence.** Bank statements that don't clearly demonstrate the five cumulative months. This is the single biggest cause of renewal denials. Permit holders who used Wise/Revolut throughout the year, or who paid for everything in cash, struggle to prove they were physically present.

**2. Income drift.** Income has dropped below the €42,000 threshold (or €32,400 for pre-April-2024 applicants). Variable freelance income that averaged €3,800/month in months one through six and €2,200/month in months seven through twelve will fail the renewal review.

**3. Maltese client contamination.** During the permit year, the holder picked up a Maltese client without realising it disqualifies them. Common scenarios: a foreign agency you work for opens a Maltese subsidiary, a foreign client moves to Malta, you start invoicing a Maltese-resident friend for consulting work.

**4. Late submission.** Submitted less than two months before expiry, or worse, after expiry. The Agency does not grant exceptions for "I was travelling" or "I forgot."

**5. Unreported tax non-compliance.** This is a newer pattern, surfacing since the January 2026 MTCA Guidelines clarified the automatic registration and filing requirements. Permit holders who didn't file a Maltese tax return in their first year are flagged at renewal.

The pattern across all five: most renewal denials are preventable, and most preventable causes are visible at month 9 or earlier. Audit your situation in month 9 against the five categories above. If anything is borderline, an agent consultation at month 10 is much cheaper than a denial at month 12.

## The four-year question

The NRP can be renewed three times, for a maximum total stay of four years. After year four, the permit expires and cannot be renewed further. This is hard.

Three options at year four:

**Leave Malta.** Most permit holders do this, either because they're ready to move on or because their plans have shifted. Schengen visa-free travel still applies based on your nationality after the permit expires.

**Reapply as a fresh NRP applicant.** Possible, but only after a full 12 months from the date your previous permit expired. The 12-month gap is mandatory. During this period you don't have residency status in Malta. Tax residency considerations may also reset.

**Switch to a different Maltese residence programme.** The MPRP (Permanent Residence Programme) has fundamentally different requirements: a €350,000+ property purchase or €12,000/year lease, €40,000 government contribution, and a clean diligence file. The income-based eligibility shifts to capital-based. For most NRP holders, this is a different programme for a different applicant profile.

A few permit holders successfully transition into Maltese employment with a regular work permit if they find a Maltese employer willing to sponsor them. This is uncommon but possible.

The practical advice: at month 30 of your overall NRP timeline (so during your second renewal year), start thinking about year four. By month 36, you should have a plan. The two-year-out timeline isn't conservative — it's the minimum needed to either prepare for an MPRP application, transition to alternative status, or organise the relocation back home.

## The renewal-month checklist

A working checklist for what to do when:

**Month 8:** Open a Maltese bank account if you haven't yet. Check your existing Maltese bank statements for clear evidence of presence. Set up direct debit for utilities to your Maltese account if possible.

**Month 9:** Audit your income against the threshold. Audit your Maltese client list (have any of your clients become Maltese-based?). Engage a Maltese tax accountant for your first year's filing. Begin requesting your police conduct certificate from your home country (4–8 weeks lead time).

**Month 10:** Begin gathering renewal documents — three months of recent bank statements, employment contract, lease agreement, insurance policy renewal quote.

**Month 10 or 11:** Submit the renewal application via the Residency Malta portal. Pay the €300 government fee from your own account.

**Month 11–12:** Wait for Letter of Approval in Principle. Submit accommodation + insurance documents within 30 working days.

**Month 12:** Receive Letter of Final Approval. Book biometrics appointment. Pay €100 for the new card.

**Month 12–13:** Old card expires; new card arrives 3–4 weeks later. There may be a brief overlap with no card in hand, but your status is regular as long as the renewal is in process.

**Month 13:** File your Maltese tax return for the previous calendar year. The 10% flat rate kicks in (year-one income exemption ends).

If you do these steps at the right time, the renewal is straightforward administrative work. If you skip the early steps and try to compress everything into month 11, the renewal becomes a stress event with real risk of denial. The renewal is the part of the NRP where front-loading effort pays the highest dividend.
