The Malta Nomad Residence Permit's official application fee is €300 per applicant. The residence card fee is another €100 paid in person at biometrics. Total mandatory government fees: €400 for a single applicant.

This is the number every immigration firm leads with. It is also misleading.

The realistic upfront cost of moving to Malta on the NRP is closer to €19,000 to €25,000 for a single applicant. None of this is hidden by the Agency or imposed unexpectedly by Malta. It is the standard cost of relocating to a new country, signing a 12-month lease, and meeting the permit's documentation requirements. The point of this article is that the brochure copy mentions only the €400, leaving most applicants to discover the rest only after they've already committed.

This guide walks through every line item, with realistic ranges based on 2026 prices.

## The full breakdown for a single applicant

| Category | Item | Realistic cost |
|---|---|---|
| Government fees | Application fee | €300 |
|  | Residence card | €100 |
| Documents | Translation and apostille | €1,500 – €3,000 |
|  | Police clearance certificate | €30 – €100 |
| Compliance | Health insurance (annual prepaid) | €500 – €1,200 |
| Professional services | Immigration agent fees | €1,500 – €3,000 |
| Property | Property agent commission | €600 – €1,400 |
|  | Lease deposit + first month | €2,400 – €4,200 |
|  | Utility deposits and setup | €200 – €400 |
|  | Bank account opening (if local) | €0 – €200 |
| Travel & arrival | Flights to Malta | €200 – €600 |
|  | Initial accommodation (15–30 days) | €1,500 – €3,000 |
| Bank balance | 5-month income buffer | €17,500 (held, not spent) |

**Total cash outflow:** approximately €8,800 to €18,200, depending on choices and origin country.
**Total liquidity required at application:** approximately €26,300 to €35,700, including the bank balance buffer.

The difference between "cash outflow" and "liquidity required" matters because the bank balance buffer isn't spent — it sits in your account as proof you can support yourself for the first five months. After approval, it's yours to use however you like.

The number "€19,700" used elsewhere on this site is a midpoint estimate of the cash outflow plus a partial buffer assumption. Treat any single figure as a rough anchor; the realistic range is what matters.

## Government fees: the only certain numbers

These are fixed and non-negotiable.

**€300 application fee** per person, including each dependant added to the application. A family of three pays €900. Payment must come from the main applicant's own bank account; payments from third-party accounts (including from your immigration agent's account) are rejected.

**€100 card fee** at biometrics, paid in person at Residency Malta's Valletta office. Card payments only — no cash. Each person added to the application pays this fee separately.

If your nationality requires an entry visa to enter Malta, the visa is a separate cost handled through the Central Visa Unit at Identità. Visa fees vary by nationality but are typically €60–€80.

## Document preparation: where timeline meets cost

The document checklist looks innocuous: passport copies, employment contract, bank statements, police certificate, marriage and birth certificates if applicable, CV, letter of intent. Most applicants assume they can pull these together in a weekend.

The reality is that every document originating outside Malta must be apostilled by the issuing country's competent authority and, if not in English, certified-translated. This step typically takes four to eight weeks and costs more than people expect.

Here's the realistic cost breakdown:

**Police clearance certificate.** €30–€100 depending on your country. Free in some jurisdictions, expensive in others. The certificate must be recent — typically issued within six months of the application date. If you're applying from a country where you no longer reside, you need a certificate from your current country of residence as well.

**Apostille on each foreign document.** €30–€100 per document, sometimes more. The list of documents requiring apostille typically includes: police certificate, marriage certificate, each child's birth certificate, employment contract or business registration documents. A single applicant with a clean document set might apostille three documents (€90–€300). A married applicant with two children apostilles seven (€210–€700).

**Certified translation.** €40–€80 per page for sworn or certified translators. Documents that come back from translation agencies often run 4–8 pages once the translator's certificate, the apostille text, and the source document are accounted for. Realistic translation cost for a single applicant: €300–€600. For a family of four: €800–€1,500.

**Courier and notary services.** €100–€300. Many origin countries require physical document handling — apostille offices that don't accept email submissions, notaries who must witness signatures in person, courier services to ship documents to Malta. These small fees add up.

A reasonable midpoint estimate: **€1,500 for a single applicant, €2,500 for a couple, €3,500 for a family of four.** The high end of the range is reached when documents originate in countries with slow apostille processes (Brazil, Russia legacy documents, India, parts of Latin America) where rush fees and re-submissions can add 50% to the bill.

## Health insurance: not optional, not cheap

The minimum coverage is €100,000, set out in Residency Malta Agency's published Health Coverage Table of Minimum Benefits. The current table dates to June 2025 and applies to all new applications submitted from 1 August 2024. The policy must be fully prepaid for one year — monthly-payment policies are not accepted. Travel insurance is not accepted either.

UK nationals are an exception: they have reciprocal access to Maltese public healthcare and don't need to purchase private insurance for the NRP. For everyone else, compliant policies from established providers (Cigna Global, Allianz Care, APRIL International, Bupa Global) typically cost:

**Single applicant, age 30–40, no pre-existing conditions:** €500–€800 for a basic compliant plan.

**Single applicant with moderate health history:** €700–€1,200.

**Couple, age 30–40:** €900–€1,800.

**Family of four:** €1,400–€2,800.

The plans that pass Residency Malta's review are the comprehensive expat plans, not the budget "digital nomad" plans (SafetyWing, Genki) which often have coverage gaps that fail the Agency's table of minimum benefits. Some of those budget plans now offer specific Malta NRP-compliant tiers — read the small print and confirm with the provider before purchasing.

The health insurance cost recurs annually. This isn't a one-time cost, it's an annual operating cost that you'll repeat at every renewal.

## Immigration agent fees: optional but recommended

Engaging a licensed agent is technically optional. The application can be submitted directly through the Residency Malta portal. In practice, almost all applicants who succeed work with an agent, and agents are who screen the seven [rejection patterns](/guides/nrp-rejection-reasons) before submission.

Realistic agent fee ranges:

**Single applicant, simple case:** €1,500–€2,500. The case is "simple" if you have a clean Schengen history, clear foreign employment, no AML flags, and a country of origin with smooth apostille processes.

**Single applicant, complex case:** €2,500–€4,000. Complexity includes prior Schengen denials, multiple income sources, business ownership requiring corporate documentation, or origin countries with slow document processes.

**Family of four:** €2,500–€5,000. Some firms charge per-person, some charge a fixed family rate. The latter is usually better value.

**Renewal years:** €500–€1,500 per year. Cheaper than the initial application but still meaningful when you renew three times across the four-year permit life.

The agent fee is what most applicants try to skip first when calculating their relocation budget. This is almost always a mistake. The fee buys preliminary due diligence (which catches issues before you've paid the €300 government fee), document checklist guidance (which prevents the 30% of rejections caused by document errors), and post-approval support (handling the lease registration, the biometrics booking, and the renewal cycle).

For most applicants with any complexity in their case, the choice isn't whether to engage an agent — it's which agent. For applicants with a clean profile, direct application is workable, and the [agent comparison guide](/guides/choosing-immigration-agent) walks through both decisions.

## Property: where Malta's housing market gets aggressive

The 12-month lease requirement is non-negotiable. Combined with Malta's tight rental market — particularly in Sliema, St Julian's, and Gzira where most nomads want to live — this puts the applicant in a weak negotiating position.

**Property agent commission.** Maltese letting agencies typically charge half a month's rent as commission, paid by the tenant on signing. On a €1,300/month Sliema flat, that's €650. On a €1,800/month St Julian's flat, €900. For higher-end lets above €2,000/month, the commission can hit €1,000–€1,400.

**Lease deposit.** Two months' rent is the standard demand, sometimes negotiable to one month for tenants with strong references. On a €1,300 flat, expect €2,600 in deposit, refundable at end of lease subject to property condition.

**First month's rent in advance.** Standard. €1,300 on a €1,300 flat.

**Combined property cost at signing:** €4,550 on a typical €1,300/month Sliema 1-bed. €6,200 on an €1,800/month St Julian's flat. Real money, paid weeks before you've even arrived in Malta.

**Utility deposits.** ARMS (water and electricity) requires a deposit when transferring the bill into your name, typically €100–€200. Internet installation often has a setup fee of €50–€100. Total utility setup: €200–€400.

The property cost is the single largest line item in the realistic budget. It's also the line where DIY applicants often save money by going direct to landlords through Facebook groups or Maltese property platforms — bypassing the agent commission. This works but takes longer and increases the risk of finding a property that doesn't pass Residency Malta's accommodation checks.

## Travel and arrival

You need to be physically present in Malta for biometrics, and the Letter of Final Approval requires temporary accommodation for the first 15–30 days while you finalise the long lease.

**Flights.** €200–€600 depending on origin country and time of year. Mediterranean summer (June–August) is more expensive; off-season much cheaper.

**Temporary accommodation.** €1,500–€3,000 for a 15–30 day stay in a serviced apartment or modest hotel. Sliema and St Julian's serviced apartments run €80–€150 per night for short stays. Gozo is cheaper but ferry logistics add friction in this period.

**Initial setup costs.** Local SIM, public transport top-up, basic groceries, the first round of meals out before you've found your kitchen. Realistic budget: €300–€600 for the first month.

## The 5-month bank balance buffer

Residency Malta expects to see proof that you can support yourself for at least five months from the application date. The standard interpretation is that you should hold at least €17,500 (5 × €3,500 monthly income threshold) in a bank account at the time of application.

This is not a fee. The money stays in your account. You don't pay it to anyone. But it has to be there at application time, which means many applicants need to either hold cash they would otherwise have invested, or borrow against assets to demonstrate the balance.

For an applicant near the €42K income floor, this is the line item that creates the most pre-application pressure. €17,500 in liquid cash is meaningful money for someone earning €3,500/month.

## How costs scale with family

Adding family members to the application multiplies several line items:

- Government fees: €300 application fee + €100 card fee per added person
- Document preparation: each person needs apostilled documents (passport, birth certificate, marriage certificate)
- Health insurance: each person needs a compliant policy
- The income threshold: stays at €42,000 — it does not increase with family size
- Bank balance buffer: stays at €17,500
- Agent fees: typically increase 30–50% for a couple, 50–100% for a family of four

Realistic incremental cost per added family member: €1,500–€3,000 in additional cash outflow. A family of four typically adds €4,500–€9,000 to the single-applicant base case.

## Costs that recur

Several costs are not one-time. Plan for them at year 2, 3, and 4:

- **Health insurance:** €500–€1,200 per person per year, recurring
- **Renewal application fees:** €300 government + €100 card per person, plus €500–€1,500 agent fee, every year
- **Annual tax filing:** €80–€200 with a Maltese accountant from year 2 onwards
- **Lease renewal:** if you stay in the same property, no commission. If you move, half-month commission applies again

A four-year stay on the NRP, including renewals, typically adds €4,000–€8,000 in recurring costs to the initial €19,000–€25,000 setup.

## The realistic total

For a single applicant, the cash outflow to obtain the permit and complete the first year in Malta is **€8,800 to €18,200**, with another €17,500 needing to be visible in your bank account at application time.

For a couple, the same numbers approximately become €13,000 to €26,000, plus the bank balance buffer.

For a family of four, €18,000 to €34,000 plus buffer.

These are conservative midpoint estimates. Costs at the high end of each range are reached when origin-country apostille is slow, document translation needs are heavy, immigration agent fees are at the premium tier, property is selected in the most expensive areas, and family size is at four.

The realistic budget for moving to Malta on the NRP is therefore not "€400 in government fees" but **roughly €20,000 of real money for a single applicant, €25,000 for a couple, €30,000+ for a family of four,** with a comparable additional sum needing to be liquid at the moment of application.

If your relocation budget can absorb that, the NRP is a workable option for the right profile. If your budget can't, the right move is either to wait until it can, or to look at alternative permits in countries with lower documentation requirements and faster timelines.
