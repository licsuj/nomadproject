The flagship guide tells you the rate. This article tells you what filing actually looks like — the forms, the portal, the deadlines, and the part where you realise the standard Maltese tax system was not designed with your specific situation in mind.

The 10% flat tax sounds simple in marketing copy. In practice, an NRP holder's tax return sits in an awkward middle ground: you're not on Maltese payroll (so no FS3 from a Maltese employer), you're not a normal self-employed person (you provide services to non-Maltese clients only), and you may or may not be a Maltese tax resident depending on how many days you spent here. The forms ecosystem expects you to fit a category. You don't quite fit any of them.

This guide is what an accountant would walk you through over coffee, written down so you can read it before you pay an accountant. We still recommend hiring one for your first year. After year one, depending on how steady your situation is, some NRP holders self-file. Most don't.

## The thing that catches almost everyone out

Tax registration is **automatic** when your residence card is issued. This was confirmed by the MTCA Guidelines published on 16 January 2026 and applies to anyone whose permit was issued on or after 1 January 2024.

What this means in practice: from the moment you collect your residence card, you have a Maltese tax file. You don't get an email confirming it. You don't get a welcome packet. The MTCA's records simply now contain your name and a tax reference number, and from that point you have a filing obligation.

Most NRP holders only discover this around month 11 or 12 of their first year, when someone mentions tax filing and they panic. By then, the deadline for the calendar year that's just ended is six months away, which feels comfortable until you realise you don't have a tax reference number, you've never used Malta's e-Services portal, and you don't have an e-ID.

Start now. The administrative steps you can take in month 2 of your permit will save you weeks of stress in month 14.

## What you actually need to file

Three things, in order of how long they take to obtain:

**An e-ID.** This is Malta's digital identity system, run by Identità (formerly Identity Malta Agency). The CFR e-Services portal — which is the online tax filing system — requires e-ID for access. Most NRP holders need to apply for one in person at an Identità office. Plan one to two weeks for issuance. If you're applying for the e-ID together with your residence card biometrics appointment, you can sometimes do both in one visit.

**A tax reference number.** This is the number assigned to your Maltese tax file when your permit was issued. To find it, you can either ask your accountant to look it up via their professional access, or contact the MTCA directly via servizz@gov.mt with your permit details. Some NRP holders find their tax reference number printed on documentation from the MTCA arriving by post in the months after their card is issued.

**Records of the income you're declaring.** For NRP holders, this is typically:
- Bank statements showing income credited from foreign employers or clients
- Your employment contract or service agreements, as evidence of the income source
- Records of any foreign tax paid on the same income (for double taxation relief)

You don't issue yourself an FS3 — that form comes from a Maltese employer to a Maltese employee, which doesn't apply to you. You document your income via bank statements and contracts instead.

## The deadline that actually matters

The Maltese tax year is the calendar year. Income earned between 1 January and 31 December of one year is reported on a return filed the following year.

**30 June** is the official filing deadline.

**31 July** is the extended deadline if you file electronically through the CFR e-Services portal.

For practical purposes, treat 31 July as your real deadline since electronic filing is the standard route for non-Maltese-employed individuals.

Late filing penalties range from €10 to €500 depending on how late and what's outstanding. They're not catastrophic, but they're a paper trail of non-compliance that gets flagged at your second renewal, where you have to submit a tax compliance declaration.

If your income arose before 1 January 2024, you don't have an NRP-related Maltese tax liability for that period (the rules took effect from 1 January 2024). For post-1-January-2024 income, the filing obligation exists from your first calendar year onwards, even during the 12-month exempt period.

## Year one — the exempt year

This is the part that confuses people most.

For the first 12 months from when your permit was issued (or from 1 January 2024, whichever is later), your authorised work income is **exempt** from Maltese tax. The exemption is automatic; you don't apply for it.

But here's the part that almost no one explains correctly: **you still file a return.** The exemption is not the same as no obligation. You report your income, claim the exemption under Subsidiary Legislation 123.210 (the Nomad Residence Permits (Income Tax) Rules), and the resulting Maltese tax due on authorised work income is zero.

Why this matters:
- It establishes a clean filing record from the start
- It documents your foreign-source income for any future tax authority queries
- It satisfies the renewal-stage compliance declaration requirement (year 2 onwards)
- It provides evidence for treaty-based double taxation relief if you also pay foreign tax

Skipping the return because "I owe zero" is the most common first-year mistake. Don't.

## Year two onwards — the 10% flat rate

Once the 12-month exemption expires, your authorised work income becomes taxable in Malta at a flat 10%. The progressive rates that go up to 35% don't apply to this income — the 10% is a separate, fixed regime under S.L. 123.210.

What "authorised work" means precisely: services provided in accordance with a contract of employment with a non-Maltese employer who has no fixed place of business in Malta, or services provided in a self-employed capacity to non-Maltese clients with no fixed place of business in Malta. Anything that doesn't fit this definition isn't "authorised work" and therefore doesn't qualify for the 10% rate.

So if you're an NRP holder and you have:
- **Foreign employer income** (your main job): 10% flat
- **Maltese rental income** (you sublet a room): 15% withholding tax under the standard rental regime, OR progressive rates if you opt out of the 15% scheme
- **Capital gains** on Maltese property or shares: standard Maltese capital gains rules apply
- **Worldwide investment income** (dividends, interest from non-Maltese sources): depends on whether you're considered Maltese tax-resident, see below

The 10% applies only to the income that qualified you for the permit. Everything else is taxed normally.

## The 183-day complication

This is the section your accountant will earn their fee on.

Under Maltese general tax law — separate from the NRP rules — an individual is considered tax-resident in Malta if they're physically present in Malta for more than 183 days in a calendar year. NRP holders meeting the 5-cumulative-month renewal threshold often inadvertently cross 183 days without thinking about it (5 months ≈ 152 days, but most permit holders are here longer than the strict minimum).

Tax residency under general law is broader than tax registration under the NRP rules. If you become a Maltese tax resident, you may have an obligation to declare income that wouldn't otherwise be reportable in Malta — including remitted foreign income, certain global income depending on domicile, and so on.

The MTCA Guidelines clarified one important point: **holding an NRP does not automatically make you a Maltese tax resident.** Tax residency is assessed separately, based on physical presence and other factors (habitual residence, economic ties, etc.). An NRP holder who carefully manages their physical presence to stay under 183 days can avoid broader tax-residency status while still benefiting from the 10% flat rate on authorised work income.

The practical implication: if you're spending most of the year in Malta, you may be subject to Maltese tax on more than just your authorised work income. If you're rotating in and out and staying under 183 days, your obligations are narrower. Your accountant needs to know your day count.

## Foreign tax credit and double taxation relief

Most NRP holders are also subject to tax in their home country, or in the country where their employer is registered. Malta has a wide network of double-taxation treaties (over 70 countries) and the MTCA Guidelines confirm that foreign tax paid on authorised work income can offset the 10% Maltese tax via double taxation relief.

To claim relief:
- Document the foreign tax paid (foreign tax assessments, employer statements, foreign tax returns)
- Submit the documentation alongside your Maltese tax return
- The Maltese tax due is reduced by the credit, subject to the relevant treaty's provisions

In practice, if you're a US citizen working remotely for a US employer, you'll likely pay US federal tax on your income. If the US tax exceeds 10%, the Maltese tax due could be entirely offset by the foreign tax credit. Whether it's fully offset depends on the treaty's specific mechanics, your domicile, and whether you're using the foreign earned income exclusion or other US-side reliefs.

Get an accountant who has handled at least three NRP filings before. Cross-border tax is the part where DIY filing goes wrong most expensively.

## The forms map — what applies, what doesn't

Most online articles about Maltese tax forms list FS3, FS4, FS5, FS7, TA22, and others without explaining which apply to whom. For NRP holders specifically, here's the clean picture:

**Forms that DO apply to you:**
- **The annual self-assessment income tax return** filed via CFR e-Services (this is the main filing)
- **Records of foreign income and foreign tax paid** as supporting documentation
- **Declaration of compliance with tax laws** (renewal years 2 and 3 only, downloaded from the Residency Malta portal)

**Forms that DON'T apply to NRP holders (despite being mentioned in general Maltese tax guides):**
- **FS3** — issued by Maltese employers to employees on Maltese payroll. NRP holders don't have a Maltese employer.
- **FS4** — payee status declaration submitted to a Maltese employer. Not relevant.
- **FS5/FS7** — employer reconciliation forms. Not relevant.
- **TA22** — provisional tax form for self-employed individuals trading IN Malta. NRP holders provide services to non-Maltese clients, so this form's framework doesn't fit.
- **TA24** — rental income at 15% flat rate. Only relevant if you sublet your accommodation, which most NRP holders don't.
- **TA26** — artistic activities. Almost never relevant.

The clean distinction: NRP holders are NOT in the FSS (Final Settlement System, Malta's payroll tax system) — the MTCA Guidelines confirm this. Your foreign employer has no Maltese payroll obligations. Your tax is settled through your own annual return, not through monthly payroll deductions.

## The actual filing process

Once you have your e-ID and tax reference number, the filing flow is roughly:

1. Log into CFR e-Services at [mtca.gov.mt](https://mtca.gov.mt) using your e-ID
2. Select "Income Tax Return" for the relevant year of assessment
3. Declare your authorised work income (typically aggregated from your bank statements)
4. Apply the 10% flat rate (or the exemption, if year one)
5. Declare any other income — rental, capital gains, foreign passive income — under the relevant standard provisions
6. Apply foreign tax credits if applicable, with supporting documentation
7. Submit and receive a confirmation receipt
8. Pay any tax due by the deadline (electronically, via the same portal)

The portal is functional but not modern. Allow 2-3 hours for your first filing if you're doing it yourself. After that it gets faster.

If your authorised work income comes through a single foreign employer in clean monthly transfers, the filing is relatively simple. If it's spread across freelance clients in multiple currencies with varying foreign tax treatments, it's not.

## What goes wrong

Five recurring patterns from NRP holders' first-year filings:

**1. Skipping the exempt-year return.** Many holders assume "exempt = nothing to file." Wrong. File the return, claim the exemption, end with zero tax due. This becomes critical at the second renewal when tax compliance is actively re-checked.

**2. Misclassifying income.** Reporting foreign employer income as "self-employed income" or vice versa changes the form treatment and can invalidate the 10% rate. Your contract and bank statement evidence has to match the income category you declare.

**3. Missing the e-ID step.** Showing up in May to file a return due in June and discovering you can't access the portal because you don't have e-ID. The application process can take 2 weeks.

**4. Day count creep.** Spending more than 183 days in Malta without realising it puts you into broader tax-residency territory. The 5-month minimum for renewal is not the same as the 183-day threshold for tax residency. You can comfortably hit one without the other, but most permit holders end up tripping the 183-day line by accident.

**5. Foreign tax credit errors.** Either missing the credit (paying twice) or claiming it incorrectly (claiming credits beyond what the treaty allows). Cross-border filing is genuinely complex; the savings are real but only if the paperwork is right.

## When you can self-file, when you can't

A reasonable rule of thumb:

**You can probably self-file if:**
- You have one foreign employer paying a monthly salary
- You're below 183 days in Malta and have no other Maltese income
- You're past year one (so the 10% rate is straightforward)
- Your home country and Malta have a clear bilateral treaty

**You should hire an accountant if:**
- It's your first year (the registration mechanics are unfamiliar)
- You're a freelancer with multiple clients across multiple countries
- You're crossing or near the 183-day threshold
- You have non-trivial foreign tax credits to claim
- You have any Maltese-source income beyond your authorised work
- You're a US citizen (the US tax filing complexity always justifies a specialist)

A reasonable Maltese accountant for NRP filings costs €600–€1,200 annually depending on complexity. For freelancers with multi-country income, €1,500–€2,500 is more realistic. The cost is deductible against your Maltese tax liability if you're careful about how you structure it.

## The compliance declaration at second renewal

Quick reminder: at your **second renewal** (going into permit year 3) and **third renewal** (going into year 4), Residency Malta requires a signed declaration confirming you've complied with all applicable Maltese tax laws and reporting obligations.

This is the moment your filing history catches up with you. If you've filed your returns properly each year, the declaration is a formality. If you've missed filings or filed incorrectly, the declaration becomes the point at which you have to either lie (sign anyway) or fix the underlying problem (file overdue returns, often with penalties).

The declaration is downloadable from the Residency Malta portal and submitted with your renewal application.

## A working timeline for year one

Here's what a clean first-year filing looks like in practice:

**Month 1–2 of permit:** Apply for e-ID at Identità. Find or request your tax reference number.

**Month 6:** Engage a Maltese accountant. Have them confirm your filing strategy and what records to keep.

**Month 9–10:** Begin organising your year's records — bank statements, foreign employer correspondence, foreign tax documentation.

**January–February of the following year:** Receive any year-end statements from your foreign employer. Reconcile your bank records.

**April–May:** Hand records to your accountant. Review the draft return.

**Before 31 July:** File electronically via CFR e-Services. Pay any tax due (typically zero in year one because of the exemption).

**Save copies of everything.** The MTCA can audit returns for several years after filing, and the documentation you have today will be inaccessible in three years.

---

The 10% flat tax is a real benefit. It's also less automatic than the marketing copy suggests. The administrative burden falls on the holder, not on a Maltese employer or payroll system, and the forms ecosystem assumes you fit a category that you don't quite fit.

Treat the first year's filing as the project that sets the pattern for years two through four. Get it right once and the subsequent years are mostly copy-paste with updated numbers.
